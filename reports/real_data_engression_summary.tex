\section{Model Architectures}

Every model is just a sampler \(\widehat{Y}=g(X,\varepsilon)\): feed in the weather
\(X\) plus a shot of random noise \(\varepsilon\) and you get one possible CCN; draw
many times and you trace out the whole distribution \(P(Y\mid X)\). All are trained the
same way (the energy-score / CRPS loss). The only thing that changes between them is how
\(X\) is read and where the noise goes in.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Built-in (vanilla).} The public \texttt{engression} model: \(X\) is
  flattened and fed to a StoNet (MLP with fresh noise concatenated at every layer).
  No time order.
  \item \textbf{LSTM + default head.} LSTM encoder \(\to\) summary \(h(X)\); one
  noise vector concatenated to \(h(X)\), then a plain MLP.
  \(\widehat{Y}=g(h(X),\varepsilon)\).
  \item \textbf{LSTM + StoNet head.} Same encoder; the head re-injects noise at
  every layer. No monotonicity.
  \item \textbf{LSTM + pre-additive head.} \(Y=g(\phi(X)+\eta)\): the encoder makes
  a low-dimensional index \(\phi(X)\), noise \(\eta\) is \emph{added}, and a
  monotone \(g\) maps to the target. The only head with the paper's extrapolation
  theory.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
In every case the noise enters late, after a deterministic encoder --- the randomness
lives in the final CCN, and noise pushed deep into a 241-step recurrence tends to get
washed out.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Data.} Eastern North Atlantic (ENA). Each sample is a $10$-day airmass
back-trajectory --- $241$ hourly steps $\times$ $22$ weather variables --- and we
predict $\log_{10}(\mathrm{CCN})$, the cloud-particle count when the air arrives.
Dropping samples with a missing target leaves $\approx 60{,}866$ trajectories.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Train/test split (``paper'' split).} The test set is six alternating months of
2022 (Jan, Mar, May, Jul, Sep, Nov); everything else trains --- $57{,}646$ train vs
$3{,}220$ test. Using whole-month blocks stops near-identical neighbouring hours from
leaking across the split. Since the test months sit between training months, this tests
\emph{interpolation}, not extrapolation.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Dimensions.} The LSTM keeps the unflattened sequence,
$X\in\mathbb{R}^{57646\times 241\times 22}$ (train). The flat (vanilla) model
instead flattens each trajectory to $241\times 22 = 5302$ features.

on the training energy loss.

\noindent
\textbf{Takeaways.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sequence structure helps.} The LSTM is calibrated while staying
  sharp and accurate; the flat model reaches coverage only with wide, inaccurate
  intervals.
  \item \textbf{Under-dispersion is the main failure.} Many configurations collapse
  to a sharp point and lose coverage; coverage \emph{anti-correlates} with
  sharpness.
  \item \textbf{What controls collapse.} Learning rate dominates (StoNet collapsed
  at lr$=0.003$, recovered at lr$=0.01$); more \emph{depth} helps, more \emph{width}
  hurts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Out-of-Sample Performance}

We measure how far each test point sits from the training data (in the LSTM's
embedding space, by $k$NN and Mahalanobis), then check whether coverage holds up as that
distance grows.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Findings.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Both heads' best runs are roughly \emph{flat} across distance
  (Figure~\ref{fig:oos}): calibration is robust across the training support.
  Under-dispersed (collapsed) configurations instead lose coverage with distance.
  \item $k$NN and Mahalanobis agree closely.
\end{itemize}

\section{Train/Test Distribution Shift}

The paper split is \emph{supposed} to be in-distribution --- let's check. One catch
first: adjacent hours are $\sim$99.7\% identical, so the $57{,}646$ train / $3{,}220$
test rows are really only a few hundred \emph{independent} episodes. So we thin to every
24th row ($2{,}402$ train / $135$ test) and describe each trajectory with 132 summary
numbers. Wildfire is a similar slice of each side ($11.1\%$ train vs $12.7\%$ test), so
the split doesn't lopside the treatment balance.

Three complementary tests ask three different questions.

\textbf{(1) Classifier two-sample test (C2ST) --- are they different, and how?}
A classifier trained to tell train-period from test-period trajectories reaches
\textbf{AUC $=0.79\pm0.02$} (linear probe $0.73$), well above the $0.5$ coin-flip line,
so the periods are clearly distinguishable. The drift is dominated by
\textbf{carbon monoxide} --- a combustion tracer --- then pressure and
marine/aerosol-source channels (Figure~\ref{fig:shift_c2st}).

\textbf{(2) MMD permutation test --- is it statistically real?}
The maximum-mean-discrepancy statistic sits $\sim$7 standard deviations beyond its
label-shuffled null: \textbf{$p=0.0008$} (the floor for $5000$ permutations), matched
by an independent energy-distance test ($p=0.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:shift_dist}a). The
shift is significant. (With enough independent episodes \emph{any} nonzero difference
becomes significant, so a small $p$ means the shift is \emph{real}, not \emph{large}.)

\textbf{(3) kNN overlap --- does the test still sit inside training territory?}
Median nearest-neighbour distances are nearly equal (train$\to$train $8.01$ vs
test$\to$train $8.36$, ratio $1.04$), and only $7.4\%$ of test episodes fall beyond the
training 95th percentile (Figure~\ref{fig:shift_dist}b). Despite the significant shift,
test episodes overwhelmingly land \emph{inside} the training support.

\paragraph{Why this matters.} Since the test still sits \emph{inside} the training data,
the effect measured where both models have support --- the ATT at wildfire weather
(Section~\ref{sec:causal}) --- is on solid ground. An ATE/ATC would instead drag a model
into the thin regions the shift opens up. That's the concrete reason we lead with the ATT.

\section{Variable Structure and Correlations}

For context, Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} scatters every variable against every other (one
point per sample, the trajectory mean), and Figure~\ref{fig:corr} boils that down to
correlations --- Pearson (linear) next to Spearman (rank). The takeaway: \textbf{no
single variable drives CCN}. The strongest is solar radiation (\texttt{SWGDN}, $+0.33$),
then cloud water, low cloud, and wind speed (all $\approx-0.25$) --- more particles on
sunny, calm, low-cloud days. Pearson and Spearman look almost identical, so these
one-to-one relationships are nearly straight-line; the real nonlinearity is in how the
variables combine. And \texttt{CO}, the variable that most separates train from test,
barely correlates with CCN on its own --- it marks \emph{when} smoky air shows up, not
how much CCN there is.

\section{Distributional Calibration Across the Sweep (PIT)}

Coverage (Section~\ref{sec:insample}) is one calibration check; PIT is the sharper one.
For each point, the PIT is the fraction of the model's predicted samples that fall below
the true CCN. If the model's distribution is right these come out uniform, so the
histogram should be \textbf{flat} --- a \textbf{U-shape} means the intervals are too
narrow (overconfident), a dome means too wide. Across all 48 configs
(Figure~\ref{fig:pit}), most are mildly U-shaped: the models are a touch overconfident
(the same under-dispersion from Section~\ref{sec:insample}), but none are badly off and
the best ones are nearly flat.

\section{Causal Findings: The Wildfire Effect (T-learner)}
\label{sec:causal}

Now the actual science: does wildfire smoke reaching ENA change CCN? We use a
\textbf{T-learner} --- train one model on wildfire trajectories, another on
wildfire-free ones --- then run \emph{both} on the same held-out wildfire-period
weather. The wildfire model gives the real ``with wildfire'' CCN; the clean model gives
the counterfactual ``what it would have been without.'' The gap between them is the
wildfire effect --- an \textbf{ATT} (effect on the treated).

\textbf{Result.} Wildfire \textbf{raises CCN by roughly 15--20\%} during wildfire periods
($\approx +40$ particles cm$^{-3}$); the whole distribution shifts up with a fatter high
tail, so extreme-CCN episodes become more common. The finding holds across three
stochastic-generator variants (Figure~\ref{fig:money}), each estimating between $+10\%$
and $+24\%$.

The three generators all make the LSTM output a \emph{distribution} by injecting Gaussian
noise; they differ in where it enters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-timestep:} a noise vector concatenated to the weather inputs, redrawn
  fresh and independent at every timestep.
  \item \textbf{Global-latent:} the same, except the noise vector is drawn once per
  trajectory and held fixed across all timesteps (one shared latent code).
  \item \textbf{Recurrent:} unlike the other two, the noise is \emph{added} to the LSTM's
  hidden state (not concatenated to the inputs) at each step, so it accumulates through
  the sequence.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{A few caveats.} This is the effect during \emph{real} wildfire periods, and it
assumes the weather variables capture everything that differs between wildfire and clean
air (ignorability). We don't have formal error bars yet --- the spread is $+10\%$ to
$+24\%$ across generators. But because it's an \textbf{ATT measured where both models
have data} (overlap holds, per the shift section), it stands on firmer ground than an
ATE/ATC that would force the data-poor wildfire model to extrapolate.
