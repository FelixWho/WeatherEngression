\section*{The question, in one paragraph}
Does wildfire smoke reaching the Eastern North Atlantic (ENA) impact the number of
cloud-condensation nuclei (CCN, the particles clouds form on)? We can't randomize
wildfires, so we lean on generative models: for each 10-day airmass trajectory we learn
the \emph{whole distribution} of CCN it could produce, then compare ``with wildfire''
against ``without'' at the same weather. This report summarizes what matters: the models
we used, whether we trust them, and what they say.

This project was motivated by Zhou et al.\footnote{S.~Zhou, D.~Qi, H.~Liu, et al., ``A Lagrangian Time-Series Machine Learning Framework for Predicting Concentrations and Exploring Drivers of Atmospheric Aerosols: Model Development and Application to Cloud Condensation Nuclei in the Marine Boundary Layer,'' AGU Fall Meeting 2024, abstract~1650614, \url{https://agu.confex.com/agu/agu24/meetingapp.cgi/Paper/1650614}; preprint: ESS Open Archive, doi:10.22541/essoar.177038612.23579844.}. Unlike the
paper, which presents a prediction task, we focus on a causal inference task.

\section*{Data and split}
Each sample is a $10$-day back-trajectory timeseries: $241$ hourly steps $\times$ $22$ weather
variables, with target $\log_{10}(\mathrm{CCN})$ at arrival. We use the month-block
(``paper'') split: six alternating months of 2022 are held out, the rest train. 
This is to mimic the split found in the motivating paper.

Two facts to carry forward: wildfire is a \emph{small minority} of the data ($\sim$11\%),
and adjacent hours are $\sim$99.7\% identical, so the tens of thousands of rows are really
only a few hundred \emph{independent} episodes.

\section*{The models we used}
Everything is an \emph{engression} model: a sampler $\widehat{Y}=g(X,\varepsilon)$ that
takes the weather $X$ plus random noise $\varepsilon$ and returns one possible CCN; draw
it many times and you trace out $P(Y\mid X)$. Training uses the energy-score (CRPS) loss,
which rewards the whole predicted distribution, not just its mean. For the observed
target $y$ and two independent draws $\widehat{Y}=g(X,\varepsilon)$,
$\widehat{Y}'=g(X,\varepsilon')$ from the model,
\[
  \mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big|\widehat{Y}-y\big|
  \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big|\widehat{Y}-\widehat{Y}'\big|.
\]
The first term pulls samples toward the truth; the second rewards spread, so the model
can't collapse to a single point. In practice we minimize its unbiased estimate over $m$
draws $\widehat{y}_1,\dots,\widehat{y}_m$:
\[
  \widehat{\mathcal{L}} \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big|\widehat{y}_i-y\big|
  \;-\; \frac{1}{2m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\big|\widehat{y}_i-\widehat{y}_j\big|.
\]

All three generators we carried into the causal analysis share one skeleton: an
\textbf{LSTM reads the 241-step trajectory}, and a small head turns the result into a
distribution over CCN. What separates them, and makes them stochastic, is
\textbf{where the noise enters the recurrence}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Per-timestep.} A fresh, independent $96$-dim noise vector is
\emph{concatenated onto the $22$ weather inputs at every one of the $241$ steps} (so the
LSTM input widens $22\!\to\!118$). Because it is re-rolled every step, this one resists
collapse the best of the three.
\item \textbf{Global-latent.} The \emph{same} wiring, except a single $96$-dim code $\varepsilon$ is
drawn \emph{once per trajectory} and concatenated to every step (the same $\varepsilon$ all the way
through), giving one shared ``latent code'' for the whole sequence.
\item \textbf{Recurrent.} The odd one out: instead of concatenating noise to the inputs,
it uses an \texttt{LSTMCell} and \emph{adds} noise directly to the hidden state after each
step, $h_t \leftarrow h_t + \mathrm{softplus}(s)\,\varepsilon_t$ (with a learned per-unit
scale $s$). Because the state carries forward, the randomness \textbf{accumulates} through
the sequence.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Do we trust the models?}
Two checks before believing any causal number.

\textbf{Calibration (PIT).} PIT stands for \emph{probability integral transform}. It
rests on the fact behind inverse-transform sampling: if $X$ is drawn from a distribution
with CDF $F$, then $F(X)$ is uniform on $[0,1]$. So we feed each observed CCN through its
own predicted CDF, and if the model's distribution is right the results should be
\textbf{uniform}. Concretely, the PIT is just the fraction of the model's predicted
samples below the true CCN (an empirical $F$). A well-calibrated model then gives a
\textbf{flat} histogram; a \textbf{U-shape} means overconfident (intervals too narrow). Across all 48 sweep configs
(Figure~\ref{fig:pit}) most are \emph{mildly} U-shaped, many are a touch overconfident. The best are nearly flat.

\textbf{Is the test weather in-distribution?} The 2022 test weather \emph{is}
significantly different from training (a classifier tells them apart at AUC $0.79$; an MMD
permutation test gives $p=0.0008$, driven mostly by carbon monoxide, a smoke tracer). But
``different'' isn't ``new'': test trajectories still sit \emph{inside} the training
region (nearest-neighbour distance ratio $1.04$; only $7.4\%$ past the training 95th
percentile, Figure~\ref{fig:shift}). \textbf{Overlap holds}.

\section*{What relates to CCN?}
No single variable drives CCN (Figure~\ref{fig:corr}). The strongest correlate is
downwelling solar radiation (\texttt{SWGDN}, $+0.33$), then cloud water, low cloud, and
wind speed (all $\approx-0.25$). In plain terms, particles show up more on sunny, calm,
low-cloud days. Pearson and Spearman come out nearly identical, so these one-to-one
relationships stay close to straight lines, and the real nonlinearity lives in how the
variables combine. \texttt{CO} is worth a note. Even though it separates train from test
more than anything else, it barely correlates with CCN by itself; what it really flags is
\emph{when} smoky air arrives, not how much CCN there is.

\section*{The result: wildfire raises CCN}
We use a \textbf{T-learner}: train one model on wildfire trajectories and another on
wildfire-free ones, then run \emph{both} on the same weather. The wildfire model gives the
real ``with smoke'' CCN; the clean model gives the counterfactual ``what it would have been
without.'' The gap is the wildfire effect. Both arms use the same stochastic LSTM
generators from the Models section, and we run the whole T-learner once per architecture:
per-timestep ($5\times128$), global-latent ($3\times128$), and recurrent (hidden $192$).
So each row below, and each effect plot, is one complete two-arm T-learner built from that
architecture.

Our headline number is the \textbf{ATE (average treatment effect)}. This averages the
effect over \emph{all} test weather, so it captures the typical impact of wildfire across
conditions.
\textbf{Wildfire raises CCN by roughly 20--30\%} ($\approx +30$ to $+40$ particles
cm$^{-3}$), and the whole distribution shifts up with a fatter high tail
(Figure~\ref{fig:money}). All three generators agree:

\textbf{ATE vs.\ ATT, what the two numbers mean.} The ATT (right column) is the effect
restricted to weather that actually occurred \emph{during} wildfire periods; it is smaller
($+10$ to $+24\%$). The ATE is larger because it also asks ``what would wildfire do to the
\emph{clean}-weather days?'', which forces the wildfire model to predict on conditions
it saw few of, so part of the gap between ATE and ATT reflects that extrapolation. Read the
ATE as the broad average effect and the ATT as the conservative, in-support lower bound.

\section*{Caveats and bottom line}
The estimate assumes the $22$ weather variables capture everything that differs between
wildfire and clean air (ignorability); there are no formal error bars yet, and the
number of independent wildfire episodes is smaller than the number of observations, so read $+20\%$ to $+31\%$ as
a range, not exact figures. Because the ATE averages over all conditions, it leans partly
on the (data-poor) wildfire model extrapolating onto clean weather.

\textbf{Bottom line:} across three different stochastic generators, wildfire smoke raises
cloud-forming particles at ENA by roughly \textbf{20--30\%} on average (ATE), or a more
conservative \textbf{10--24\%} restricted to wildfire conditions (ATT). The models are
reasonably calibrated and the test weather stays in-support, so the direction and rough
size are on solid ground.
